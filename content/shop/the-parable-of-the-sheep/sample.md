### The Shepherd and the Flock

In the first warmth of spring, when the mud still held the hoofprints of winter and grass had that bright young color that makes even a tired old animal feel young again, a flock of sheep grazed in a wide and generous land.

They were not clever sheep. They were ordinary sheep — which is to say they were for appetite, comfort, and closeness. They liked to eat. They liked to bleat. They liked to press shoulder to shoulder at night and feel the shared heat of a hundred bodies, safe in the dark.

And they had a shepherd.

He was not always present, and he did not hover, and he did not speak much. The flock did not understand most of what he did. What they understood was simple: when wolves came, the shepherd came faster. When the wolves circled, the shepherd’s staff flashed. When the wolves tested the edges of the flock, the shepherd’s dogs snapped their teeth and drove them back into the brush.

The sheep learned a lesson that sheep cannot help but learn from the best shepherds — they learned that they were safe.

In early spring the shepherd had already prepared them for the year. He had walked the boundary lines. He had checked the water. He had brought salt blocks and set them in places that made the flock move in a rhythm instead of crowding into disease and muck. He had cut back the thorn patches and opened paths through the reeds. He was not dramatic. It was the kind you barely notice, that feels invisible.

When he finished, the flock grew happy and fat.

And then, one night, the sky broke.

A great flash appeared above the hills. It was not lightning. It was too clean and too sudden, like the world had been photographed and the shutter snapped. The sheep looked up at once, startled into unity by the same flashing bright fear.

Then came the boom.

It rolled through the earth like a giant wheel. The ground shook. The water in the troughs trembled. Birds exploded from the trees and vanished into the skies.

The flock closed its eyes in fright and huddled together. Lambs pressed into ewes. Rams lowered their heads and braced themselves against the ground.

When the trembling ended, there was only wind.

The shepherd was gone.

The next morning the sheep found no tracks. No blood. No struggle. Just an empty field.

The flock stood in the same place for a long time, blinking at the sun, waiting for someone to tell them what to do.

No one did.

So the sheep did what sheep do when the world stops.

They ate.

### The Plentiful Land

In the time that followed, the land stayed plentiful.

The grass kept growing, spring after spring. The water ran cold out of the hills. The lambs arrived on schedule. The flock grew.

Even the wolves did not come.

They watched from a distance. They moved like gray smoke along the tree line. They tested the wind.

And then they backed away.

The shepherd had defeated them so many times that the sight of the flock itself had become a warning. Some wounds become stories. Some stories become instincts. The wolves had learned that this flock was trouble. They did not see a hundred tender bodies. They saw a place where pain waited.

The flock grazed freely and grew comfortable in the shepherd’s old shadow.

At night the older sheep sometimes spoke of him.

“There was a staff,” said one.

“There were dogs,” said another.

“I remember the sound of his boots,” said a third, and the others stared at him because boots were beyond sheep vocabulary.

The young sheep listened. The shepherd became a bedtime story. Years passed.

Then more years passed.

The flock fed and bred and endlessly grew and grew and ate and bleated. They filled the valleys. They spilled into the meadows beyond the old boundary stones. They blurred into one another until a single sheep could hardly be made out from the herd.

A sheep does not recognize limits. For sheep, tomorrow is tomorrow is tomorrow is tomorrow. Today is today, and yesterday is not.
