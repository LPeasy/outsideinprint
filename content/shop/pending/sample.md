<h3 id="prologue">Prologue</h3>
<h4 id="section">1991</h4>
<p>The television made the desert green.</p>
<p>Green sky, green road, green smoke rising from a building no one at
home had entered. Green men ran across the screen with no faces. The
carpet shocked the backs of Evan's knees. His cereal had gone soft in
the bowl and tasted like sugar, spoon metal, and milk going warm.</p>
<p>His mother folded laundry on the couch. His father stood behind her
with his tie loosened, holding a coffee mug from the insurance company
that no longer used his desk. On the screen, a retired colonel drew
arrows across the night with a yellow marker. The arrows knew where they
were going. The bombs arrived without sound until the anchor gave them
one.</p>
<p>Outside, the subdivision sprinklers ticked in the dark. Wet grass
breathed through the window screen. A neighbor's garage door rose and
fell with a chain-smoker's cough. Somewhere beyond the cul-de-sac, a dog
barked at the same interval, as if reading from a schedule.</p>
<p>"Surgical," the colonel said.</p>
<p>His father said, "Clean."</p>
<p>His mother said, "Don't sit so close."</p>
<p>At school the next morning, every child got a yellow ribbon made from
construction paper. The glue smelled sweet and chemical. The ribbon
stuck to his thumb. The teacher rolled a cart to the front and the
television's gray face bloomed again. The class watched bright shapes
fall onto dark shapes. The teacher said brave, careful, necessary.</p>
<p>He raised his hand.</p>
<p>"If it's clean, why is there smoke?"</p>
<p>The teacher checked the television, the clock, then the other
children.</p>
<p>"That is not what clean means here," she said.</p>
<p>At lunch the pizza was square and orange at the edges. Milk cartons
sweated on the trays. A poster over the trash cans said CHARACTER COUNTS
in red block letters.</p>
<p>That spring, his father came home before dinner three times in one
week. The first time he said transition. The second time he said
package. The third time he did not say anything and ate standing at the
sink, pressing two fingers into the white bread until the mayonnaise
came through.</p>
<p>His mother bought a floral dress for church and cut coupons with
small silver scissors. The blades made a clean crisp sound through
paper. She said they were blessed. She said the neighborhood was stable.
She said his father had options. Her voice had a shine on it, like the
furniture polish she sprayed on Saturdays until the house smelled like
lemons over dust.</p>
<p>At the bank, a man with a red tie gave his father a folder and tapped
the folder as if the answer were inside. In the lobby, a plastic plant
leaned toward a sun it could not use. On a vinyl chair, Evan ran his
fingers along a seam split open by other people's waiting.</p>
<p>"It's a voluntary separation," the man said.</p>
<p>His father nodded.</p>
<p>In the parking lot, heat lifted off the hoods of cars. The boy
smelled asphalt and gasoline and his father's aftershave, sharp as cut
wood.</p>
<p>"Did you volunteer?" he asked.</p>
<p>His father opened the car door too hard. The metal cracked against
the stop.</p>
<p>"Get in," he said.</p>
<p>That weekend his mother held a shirt against Evan's chest under
department-store light.</p>
<p>"You are growing so fast," she said.</p>
<p>The tag scratched his neck. His father stood by a rack of belts and
looked at the floor.</p>
<p>The shirt was too small.</p>
<p>His mother bought it anyway.</p>
<p>The bag swung from her wrist all the way to the car.</p>
<h4 id="section-1">2026</h4>
<p>The summer arrived with a taste of pennies.</p>
<p>Smoke came from somewhere else and belonged to everyone by noon. It
entered offices through sealed windows, settled on tongues, grayed the
sun, and made the parking lots smell like wet ash and hot brakes.
Weather apps sent red banners. The sky had the color of an old bruise
rubbed thin.</p>
<p>In a library multipurpose room, Evan stood against the wall with the
old assumptions matrix still on his laptop.</p>
<p>On the screen, Beachtown was blue water, green features, and one pale
line crossing land it did not own. The colors made the plan look calmer
than the record beneath it.</p>
<p>"Can this actually be built?" a man asked.</p>
<p>At the lectern, the answer moved through stages, analysis,
coordination, and a yes or no that had not yet become supportable. The
man remained standing after the answer ended.</p>
<p>Evan could have opened the appendix. He could have named what the
line crossed and what the partner had not promised. Part of him wanted
to help. Another part wanted every face to turn and admit that he had
been right.</p>
<p>He stayed against the wall.</p>
<p>A woman with silver hair asked what would make the study stop. The
answer came back as milestones. She lowered her head and wrote on a
comment card.</p>
<p>The next afternoon, a ship carried Evan and Leah away from the same
smoky coast.</p>
<p>At lunch, her father asked about the future as if it were a number
waiting to be entered. Leah kept one hand near her water glass. Her
mother smiled toward the center of the table.</p>
<p>Evan knew how to fill an open future with reasonable terms. When
someone asked what came next, every face waited for an answer.</p>
<p>He looked at the blue water before he spoke.</p>
<h3 id="part-i-the-first-day">Part I: The First Day</h3>
<p>August 2023.</p>
<h4 id="chapter-1-the-badge">Chapter 1: The Badge</h4>
<p>On his first morning at the agency, the badge still smelled like warm
plastic.</p>
<p>No one was waiting for him to speak. The doors, machines, screens,
and guards asked only that he pass through correctly.</p>
<p>It came out of the machine with his last name printed a little high,
the M almost touching the blue line at the top. MARKHAM. Below it,
TEMPORARY. Below that, a bar code no one had explained to him. The
security guard slid it through a laminator that sighed and brightened
and made the plastic soft enough to curl at the corners.</p>
<p>"Keep it visible," the guard said.</p>
<p>Evan clipped it to his shirt pocket. The clip bit through the cotton
Leah had ironed the night before. He could still smell the starch when
he moved. The guard station was cold and smelled of old carpet and
coffee poured too early into a paper cup.</p>
<p>The guard checked the monitor, Evan, then the monitor again.</p>
<p>"First day?"</p>
<p>"Yes."</p>
<p>"Elevators are to the left. Don't hold the doors. They get
angry."</p>
<p>Evan thought he meant people.</p>
<p>The elevator arrived with a bell that sounded apologetic. Inside, the
brushed steel walls reflected four pale versions of him: new shirt, new
badge, new leather belt with the stiff crease still in it, new notebook
under his arm. Leah had bought the notebook. Black cover, elastic strap,
small pocket in the back. He had told her he did not need it. She had
put it in the cart anyway, beside socks and a six-pack of pens.</p>
<p>"For your federal thoughts," she had said.</p>
<p>He had laughed because she had.</p>
<p>She had said it while her phone lit in the child seat of the cart.
Her mother again. Then her supervising attorney. Then her father. Then
her mother again.</p>
<p>The supervisor's message was about a plea memo due the next morning.
Could we make the risk section less sharp? The elected State Attorney
gets nervous when the first page sounds like bad news.</p>
<p>Leah had typed Sure, I can soften it, then looked at the word soften
until the checkout clerk asked whether she had a rewards number.</p>
<p>Her mother wanted to know if Evan understood that Leah did not want a
courthouse thing. Then she praised courthouses carefully enough to
condemn them totally. Her father had offered to handle the deposit if
the good ballroom was still available, which made it feel less available
to Leah. Leah turned a pack of pens over in her hand until it slipped
from her grasp and then replied that Evan understood.</p>
<p>She had not said she wanted the wedding: the room, the aisle, the
people arranged and presented. She put the pens in the cart and
pretended to smile. She turned to Evan and said I can't wait to marry
you!</p>
<p>In the basket beside the pens was a pharmacy bag folded twice at the
top. Evan had picked it up to make space for socks. Leah had taken it
back and set it under her purse without looking at him.</p>
<p>Now the notebook pressed against his chest as his heart skipped a
beat.</p>
<p>On the sixth floor, a woman from Human Resources met him near a
framed poster of a dredge bucket above the words DELIVERING TOGETHER.
Her handshake was dry and practiced. Her name was Paula, and the name
tag hanging from her neck had faded to the color of weak tea.</p>
<p>"Welcome aboard," she said.</p>
<p>She led him through glass doors that opened after she pressed her
badge against a black square. The square beeped. A lock clicked inside
the wall. The doors parted with a little friction, as if the building
had considered him and decided to allow the day to proceed.</p>
<p>The office smelled like floor wax, printer heat, and microwave soup.
Low partitions ran in rows across the floor. Computer monitors glowed
with calendars and maps and inboxes. On one desk, a hard hat held
packets of instant oatmeal. On another, a small fan turned back and
forth over a mug full of pens. Somewhere behind a partition, someone
laughed, then stopped suddenly, then laughed again more quietly.</p>
<p>Paula gave him forms.</p>
<p>Direct deposit. Emergency contact. Network agreement. Ethics
acknowledgment. Records management. Travel card. Benefits enrollment.
Telework certification. IT acceptable use. New employee checklist. The
pages were warm from a copier and smelled faintly of toner. She showed
him where to sign and initial and date. He wrote the date carefully each
time, making the numbers the same size. By the sixth form, the side of
his hand was gray with ink.</p>
<p>"You'll get the hang of the acronyms," Paula said.</p>
<p>On the wall behind her, a chart mapped the project lifecycle from
idea to construction. Every box had an arrow. Every arrow knew where it
was going.</p>
<p>"Some of them are intuitive," she said. "Some of them just are."</p>
<p>He held still to see if this was a joke. She tilted her head to the
right and smiled as if it had been one long ago.</p>
<p>The laptop came in a cardboard box with a white asset tag on the lid.
Paula cut the tape with a key. Inside, the machine slept in foam. It
smelled like new plastic, batteries, and unopened packaging. The screen
asked him to change a password he had not used. Then it asked him to
accept terms he had not read. Then it asked him to log in again.</p>
<p>"You'll have to do your cyber training before the network gives you
full access," Paula said.</p>
<p>"How do I access the training?"</p>
<p>"Through the network."</p>
<p>She gave it a second, then clicked her mouse.</p>
<p>"We'll get you a workaround."</p>
<p>At ten, he took an oath in a conference space with three other new
employees and a flag standing in the corner. One was an engineer with
salt on his boots. One was a realty specialist who smelled like rain and
cigarette smoke. One was a student intern whose suit sleeves swallowed
his wrists. Paula read the words from a laminated sheet. Evan repeated
them with the others, line by line, while the air conditioner knocked
once in the ceiling.</p>
<p>The words had weight. Support. Defend. Faithfully discharge. Well and
faithfully.</p>
<p>He liked that part. The repetition. The old shape of it. A sentence
with a body under it.</p>
<p>Afterward, the intern said, "That was intense."</p>
<p>The realty specialist said, "You get used to everything,
eventually."</p>
<p>Back at his cubicle, his phone buzzed beside the laptop box.</p>
<p>His mother had sent a picture of a coffee mug on her kitchen counter.
The mug said PROUD MOM in blue letters, and behind it the microwave
clock showed the wrong time.</p>
<p>First day!!! Federal service looks good on you. Smile. Be positive.
We are all so proud.</p>
<p>Evan touched the message with his thumb but did not answer. The three
exclamation points seemed to demand something from him.</p>
<p>At noon, Ben Salter found him standing by the copier, watching a red
light blink above a paper tray he could not open.</p>
<p>"You must be Markham."</p>
<p>Ben was younger than Evan expected from the emails, or older in a way
that did not use the face. He had a soft blue tie loosened at the neck,
a coffee mug with a crack through the faded logo, and the relaxed
alertness of someone who knew where all exits were and did not want to
use them yet.</p>
<p>"Evan," Evan said.</p>
<p>"Ben. Economics. Plans. Occasional hostage negotiation. You eat
yet?"</p>
<p>"No."</p>
<p>"Good. Don't start with cafeteria chicken. It'll give the whole place
too much power."</p>
<p>Ben walked him past cubicles and doors and storage cabinets labeled
with words that sounded legal by accident. REVIEW COPIES. ENVIRONMENTAL.
REALTY. COST. BRIEFING BOOKS. PARTNER FILES. The carpets changed color
where old walls had been removed. Framed project photos lined the hall:
levees, beaches, pumps, locks, seawalls, ribbon cuttings, men in orange
vests pointing at water.</p>
<p>"You'll sit over here," Ben said.</p>
<p>Evan's cubicle had a monitor, a docking station, a keyboard with
shiny keys, a chair that sank too far, and a whiteboard with three ghost
words left from the last person. He could read only the middle one:
ALIGN.</p>
<p>Someone had left a binder clip in the top drawer and one mint wrapped
in green foil.</p>
<p>"This is nice," Evan said.</p>
<p>Ben took in the chair, the blank wall, the dead plant in the corner
by the file cabinet.</p>
<p>"Ambitious take. I like your attitude."</p>
<p>At the kitchenette, Ben showed him which coffee machine still worked
and how to tilt a mug at just the right angle to avoid a spill. The
machine coughed, paused, released a dark inch of liquid, then a lighter
inch, then steam like smoke off a piece of burnt toast.</p>
<p>The coffee tasted scorched. Evan swallowed it and said thanks.</p>
<p>Allison Roark came to get him at one.</p>
<p>She was compact, composed, and dressed in a navy jacket too formal
for the day but exactly right for herself. Her hair was pulled back
tight enough that not one strand had escaped the day. She carried no
paper. Her badge was turned backward on its lanyard.</p>
<p>"Evan. Welcome. I'm Allison."</p>
<p>"Good to meet you."</p>
<p>"We're glad to have you. Ben behaving?"</p>
<p>"Only in the broadest sense," Ben said.</p>
<p>Allison smiled with her mouth and glared with her eyes.</p>
<p>Her office had a window facing another wing of the building. The view
was glass looking at glass. On her desk were three neat stacks of
folders, a closed laptop, a framed photo of two boys on a soccer field,
and a mug full of unsharpened pencils.</p>
<p>"I know first days are mostly systems access and forms," she said.
"But I want to get you into project work quickly. Best way to
learn."</p>
<p>Evan opened the notebook. The first page still had only the date and
a line under it.</p>
<p>"That sounds good."</p>
<p>"We have a study you can sit in on this afternoon. Beachtown, U.S.A.
High visibility. Partner-sensitive. Still developing."</p>
<p>He wrote:</p>
<p>Beachtown.</p>
<p>High visibility.</p>
<p>Partner-sensitive.</p>
<p>Still developing.</p>
<p>"Your background in economics will be useful," she said. "Mostly
listening today. Track assumptions. Get a feel for how the team is
framing the remaining issues."</p>
<p>"Remaining issues," he said, writing it. He doodled a traintrack in
the right margin that spelled out the word A-S-S-U-M-P-T-I-O-N-S.</p>
<p>"Right. You'll hear a lot of history. Don't feel like you need to
solve it on day one."</p>
<p>"What is the question?"</p>
<p>Allison blinked.</p>
<p>"How to keep the study moving in a responsible way."</p>
<p>He wrote that too.</p>
<p>Responsible way.</p>
<p>The words lay cleanly on the page. He traced a swooping meandering
line from the word responsible over to the train tracks.</p>
<p>At two forty-five, a calendar invitation appeared on his laptop.
BEACHTOWN STUDY TEAM - FORMULATION ALIGNMENT. The invite had nineteen
names, two attachments, four acronyms in the location field, and no
agenda. A blue banner at the top said the meeting began in fifteen
minutes.</p>
<p>Ben leaned over the partition.</p>
<p>"You get the Beachtown invite?"</p>
<p>"Yes."</p>
<p>"Good project to learn on."</p>
<p>"Why?"</p>
<p>Ben smiled, looked toward Allison's closed door, then back at Evan
and winked.</p>
<p>"Everything is in there."</p>
<p>"Everything?"</p>
<p>"You'll see."</p>
<p>The meeting space was three doors down from Allison's office. Someone
had already turned the lights halfway off so the screen would look
brighter. A long cold work surface ran through the center, with a power
strip down the middle like a spine. Chairs waited around it, all the
same, all slightly too low. On the wall, a map of South Florida held
everything in blue and green.</p>
<p>Evan took a chair near the end. He opened the notebook. He wrote the
meeting title exactly as it appeared on the invite.</p>
<p>BEACHTOWN STUDY TEAM - FORMULATION ALIGNMENT.</p>
<p>Then he clicked his pen and left it ready.</p>
