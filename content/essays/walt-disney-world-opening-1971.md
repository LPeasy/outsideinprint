*Brands, Business, and Consumer Choice · Disney World Development, Part One: The Florida Project*

In 1927, Walt Disney's Oswald the Lucky Rabbit was headed for movie theaters in a series of short comedies. A [Universal advertisement](https://archive.org/details/universalweekly100movi_4/page/n476/mode/1up) offered theater operators twenty-six one-reel cartoons, naming Disney, Winkler Productions, and Universal. Behind the drawings stood a business arrangement: someone had to produce the films, someone had to distribute them, and theater owners had to book them.

![A 1927 Universal advertisement shows Oswald holding a bag marked with a dollar sign beside theater-owner testimonials.](oip-image:essays/walt-disney-world-opening-1971/oswald)

*Oswald advertised to theater owners in Universal Weekly, December 10, 1927, page 1. The published drawing and sales copy link Walt Disney, Winkler Productions, and Universal. [Original issue](https://archive.org/details/universalweekly100movi_4/page/n476/mode/1up); [scan and reuse record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Oswald_the_Lucky_Rabbit_-_Dec_1927_UW.jpg).*

By June 1928, Oswald was being produced at a new Hollywood studio established by Charles Mintz, with George Winkler directing its staff of twenty-five cartoonists and animators. [*Film Daily* reported the change](https://archive.org/details/filmdaily4344newy/page/n1250/mode/1up). That November, Walt was preparing a sound-cartoon series around Mickey Mouse. The [announced subjects](https://archive.org/details/filmdaily4546newy/page/n1267/mode/1up) included *Plane Crazy* and *Steamboat Willie*.

Mickey reached New York's Colony Theatre that month. A [November 20 report](https://archive.org/details/filmdaily4546newy/page/1305/mode/1up) described the screenings and the successful use of the cartoon's soundtrack with the theater's equipment. Five days later, a [reviewer praised the animation, music, and sound effects](https://archive.org/details/filmdaily4546newy/page/1343/mode/1up), recommending bookings in equipped theaters while noting that distribution remained unsettled. The early Mickey business was being worked out as audiences began to see it.

![Six pencil story panels for Plane Crazy show Mickey studying a flying manual, an airplane, and a Charles Lindbergh book.](oip-image:essays/walt-disney-world-opening-1971/mickey)

*Story drawings for Plane Crazy (1928), credited to Ub Iwerks. Graphite and colored pencil, sheet 1 of 6. [Library of Congress, LC-DIG-ppmsca-57586](https://www.loc.gov/item/2018662265/).*

Nearly three decades later, the Disney name was attached to a place people could enter. The *Disneyland* television program began on ABC in October 1954; the California park opened in July 1955. ABC's corporate parent held a substantial ownership share in Disneyland and helped finance its expansion. Roy Disney's [1955 annual report, pages 3-5](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1955/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201955.pdf) also described television production costing more than its direct income, while management credited the programs with generating business elsewhere. Films, broadcasting, and the park could support one another, but each required money.

## Choosing the Florida site

Florida offered room to attempt something larger. An earlier proposal had already combined a park with a planned community: a [December 1959 study for WED Enterprises, page 1](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1132&context=buzzprice#page=6) described WED, NBC, RCA, and landowner John D. MacArthur exploring a "City of Tomorrow" near Palm Beach. Those were prospective participants in that proposal; the study does not make them financiers of the resort eventually built near Orlando.

By January 1964, Disney's consultants were comparing the Ocala and Orlando areas. Their [study, pages 2, 5-8, and 11-12](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/buzzprice/158/) revisited an earlier preference for Ocala, which had offered strong exposure to passing tourists. New highways were giving Orlando comparable access to that traffic. Its larger economic base, workforce, and air connections strengthened its position. The consultants examined available acreage, prices, drainage, and road access. They were looking for land on which a large development could work.

Disney representatives began assembling property in early 1964. By October 1965, the company reported acquiring 27,443 acres for just over $5 million. Its [1965 annual report, pages 23-24](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_f7943951cd054578ac6486f6490e0dcc.pdf#page=25) emphasized Florida's established tourism, access to eastern visitors, and enough land to control the immediate surroundings and allow expansion. The theme park would occupy part of a much larger resort, with hotels, recreation, and transportation linked across the property, as the later [1969 resort plan](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_6a193a0df52c464e8906206bd932f43f.pdf#page=2) showed.

![A 1954 aerial photograph shows lakes, vegetation and cleared areas in the future Walt Disney World resort area.](oip-image:essays/walt-disney-world-opening-1971/land-before-disney)

*The future resort area southwest of Orlando in a Soil Conservation Service aerial photograph, March 1, 1954, before Disney's land purchases. [National Archives, 595120](https://docsteach.org/document/aerial-disney-world/). Public domain.*

When Walt and Roy publicly discussed Florida on November 15, 1965, a [reporter asked Walt why they had chosen central Florida](https://assets.super.so/08f84834-5ca2-4f09-967e-cb4adbb8c4fe/files/12bad40d-180c-47ee-822f-d31fea5e206c.pdf#page=37). He cited "the lands available, the freeway routes coming from all directions." Disney had received offers of free land elsewhere, he said, but those offers had not settled the choice. Later in the same conference, Roy explained that a decade of Disneyland experience had improved the company's [standing with bankers](https://assets.super.so/08f84834-5ca2-4f09-967e-cb4adbb8c4fe/files/12bad40d-180c-47ee-822f-d31fea5e206c.pdf#page=47).

![Walt Disney, Governor Haydon Burns and Roy Disney sit behind microphones at the Florida announcement.](oip-image:essays/walt-disney-world-opening-1971/announcement)

*Walt Disney, Governor Haydon Burns, and Roy Disney, left to right, at the Florida announcement on November 15, 1965. Florida Development Commission photograph, [State Archives of Florida / Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/2021669931/).*

Taxes mattered to the commitment that followed. After acquiring the land, Disney listed tax treatment, municipal arrangements, zoning, roads, water control, and other services as unresolved conditions of development. Its [1965 report, page 24](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_f7943951cd054578ac6486f6490e0dcc.pdf#page=26) called the application of state taxes the most important outstanding issue. At the announcement, Governor Haydon Burns discussed agricultural land classification and how sales taxes would apply to the design, engineering, and prototypes behind custom attractions. These were [questions being negotiated](https://assets.super.so/08f84834-5ca2-4f09-967e-cb4adbb8c4fe/files/12bad40d-180c-47ee-822f-d31fea5e206c.pdf#page=35). Road access, tourist traffic, and available acreage helped determine the location. Tax treatment and governing arrangements remained conditions of developing it.

Walt was considering how a family might spend several days on the property. In [June 1965 planning notes](https://assets.super.so/08f84834-5ca2-4f09-967e-cb4adbb8c4fe/files/12bad40d-180c-47ee-822f-d31fea5e206c.pdf#page=87), he discussed a theme park that would draw people in, with accommodation and recreation encouraging them to remain. His proposed range extended to trailers and sleeping-bag areas. Economics Research Associates approached the same problem through a visitor's limited time and money. Its [October 1965 economic master plan](https://fileserver-az.core.ac.uk/download/236314301.pdf) recommended comparatively inexpensive water recreation alongside the principal attractions. A longer stay could bring Disney more revenue, but visitors would need enough to do within their vacation budgets.

## The working city Walt proposed

Walt's ambitions extended to a working community. EPCOT, the Experimental Prototype Community of Tomorrow, would involve homes, employment, schools, transportation, and participating industries. The company's [1967 Project Florida booklet](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_95503446f8c346fba334b9b8f4f1344c.pdf#page=16) described homes for employees of Disney World and the companies in its industrial park. His [presentation notes](https://assets.super.so/08f84834-5ca2-4f09-967e-cb4adbb8c4fe/files/12bad40d-180c-47ee-822f-d31fea5e206c.pdf#page=96) followed a resident from a neighborhood into the center aboard a WEDway transit vehicle, then onto a monorail to reach the industrial park. The [community diagrams in Disney's Project Florida booklet](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_95503446f8c346fba334b9b8f4f1344c.pdf#page=13) gave the proposal a physical shape. It was a plan for daily life as well as recreation. The theme park and vacation facilities would form part of a much wider undertaking.

There were older examples of businesses helping to plan places to live as well as places to work. A [1926 Hershey Chocolate Corporation booklet](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Story_of_Chocolate_and_Cocoa_Booklet_-_NARA_-_18558585_(page_3).jpg) described the Pennsylvania town's factory, laid-out streets, homes, and gardens, and said most houses belonged to their occupants. [Its following pages](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Story_of_Chocolate_and_Cocoa_Booklet_-_NARA_-_18558585_(page_4).jpg) advertised schools, clubs, and recreation. In England, architect W. Alexander Harvey's [1906 account of Bournville](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/73532/73532-h/73532-h.htm#Page_9) described cottages with gardens, schools, and open spaces near Cadbury's chocolate works. The estate had passed to a trust in 1900, and its homes were open to people working elsewhere.

Ford's settlement at Fordlandia in Brazil offers another comparison. Photographs from 1930-1933 recorded [employee housing](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/artifact/6649) and a [school](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/artifact/41156). EPCOT likewise joined housing to employment and placed transport, schools, and services within a coordinated plan. It carried the industrial-community idea into a proposed city serving several companies, with new systems tested in residents' daily lives.

Preparations advanced during 1966. The company [reported forming the Reedy Creek Drainage District](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_c54a7c578272431285b922d9ec3fabfc.pdf#page=27), continuing preliminary design work, and meeting representatives of more than sixty-five major American industries.

## Roy and the first phase

Walt died on [December 15, 1966](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1967-pt3/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1967-pt3-2-1.pdf#page=38). In a [signed message added to the already-prepared annual report](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_c54a7c578272431285b922d9ec3fabfc.pdf#page=1), Roy told shareholders and employees that Florida would proceed, pointing to the company's creative and management organization. He had already been involved in its financing. He now had to carry that work through the loss of the person most closely identified with the plans.

Government arrangements also took shape. The [May 1967 Reedy Creek Improvement District law](https://www.oversightdistrict.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/RCID-Charter.pdf) expanded the earlier drainage organization and granted powers over infrastructure, development regulation, and financing. Supervisor elections were tied to landownership, with votes allocated by acreage. Disney's extensive holdings therefore carried influence over the government serving the property.

![Governor Claude Kirk signs Disney-related legislation at a desk while Roy Disney and other officials stand behind him.](oip-image:essays/walt-disney-world-opening-1971/roy-and-legislation)

*Governor Claude Kirk signs Disney-related legislation with Roy Disney among those behind him, May 12, 1967. Photograph by Karl E. Holland. [State Archives of Florida, C069089DE](https://floridamemory.com/items/show/80377). Public domain.*

Roy made a continuing executive commitment. His [December 1967 shareholder letter](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1967/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201967.pdf?download=1#page=21) reported a new five-year contract as president, chairman, and chief executive. The following year, he transferred the presidency to Donn Tatum while remaining chairman and chief executive. Roy explained that this would give him more time for Florida, particularly its financial planning. His stated approach was to develop the property in stages within the company's financial capacity. Joseph Fowler headed engineering and construction, while Card Walker directed operations. The [1968 report, page 1](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1968/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201968.pdf?download=1#page=3) described an undertaking with responsibilities spread across an executive team.

The [1968 report, pages 7-8](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1968/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201968.pdf?download=1#page=10), set out the development sequence. It identified the entertainment-vacation complex scheduled for 1971 as Phase One: the theme park, resort hotels, and land and water recreation. The industrial park and EPCOT belonged to the longer-range development. This gave Roy a defined first undertaking to finance and complete, while the working city remained a later proposal.

## Financing the resort

The district's financing powers faced a court challenge. In [*State v. Reedy Creek Improvement District*](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1968/37569-0.html), the Florida Supreme Court considered proposed drainage revenue bonds of up to $12 million. The State argued that the works served private Disney interests. In November 1968, the court upheld the financing, finding sufficient public purpose in the drainage improvements and their anticipated wider benefits. These bonds were to be paid from project revenues, without a general tax pledge. The decision validated a financing mechanism; it does not establish how much was sold or who bought it.

Disney was raising money as those arrangements took shape. A [January 1968 offering notice](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-BC/Broadcasting-Magazine/BC-1968/1968-01-22-BC.pdf#page=57) advertised $40 million in convertible debentures, led by Kidder, Peabody and Lehman Brothers. In 1969, the same firms led a further $50 million offering whose proceeds were designated for Florida. The [SEC's contemporary notice](https://www.sec.gov/news/digest/1969/dig021769.pdf#page=1) identifies the offering and purpose. These securities began as debt, with terms allowing investors to exchange them for shares.

![A contemporary SEC notice names Lehman Brothers and Kidder, Peabody as lead underwriters of a proposed fifty-million-dollar Disney debenture sale for the Florida complex.](oip-image:essays/walt-disney-world-opening-1971/financing)

*The SEC recorded Disney's proposed $50 million convertible-debenture sale and its intended use for the Florida complex. Detail from [SEC News Digest, February 17, 1969, page 1](https://www.sec.gov/news/digest/1969/dig021769.pdf#page=1).*

The underwriting list extended beyond the two lead firms. Goldman, Sachs, First Boston, Lazard Frères, and Paribas Corporation appeared among the institutions selling the [1969 issue](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-BC/BC-1969/1969-03-10-BC.pdf#page=78). These names establish the intermediaries involved. The notices do not identify every ultimate investor, their nationality, or their family connections.

Banks supplied another source of financial capacity. Disney's [1970 report, page 22](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1970/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201970.pdf?download=1#page=24) identified Bank of America and seven Florida banks as committing to make up to $50 million available. The report gives the credit ceiling without establishing that Disney had borrowed the full amount. In 1971, a stock sale raised about $71.8 million after expenses, and Disney issued another $100 million in convertible debt. The [annual report records those completed transactions](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=24); the [SEC disclosure](https://www.sec.gov/news/digest/1971/dig061171.pdf#page=3) says the latter issue would principally support capital spending at both Walt Disney World and Disneyland. It cannot all be counted as Florida construction money.

The company also drew financial resources from its operating businesses. Its [1971 financial statements, page 30](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=30) list operations alongside new stock and debt as sources supporting its investment program.

Roy was combining resources earned by the existing business with money raised from shareholders, bond investors, and available bank credit. U.S. Steel's hotel arrangement and the district's infrastructure financing had their own terms. These were different ways of funding development, so their announced amounts do not add up to a Florida construction bill.

On the eve of opening, Disney's [September 30, 1971, employee bulletin](https://static.wixstatic.com/media/5db718_eb418a1928804d06b98f89bd135fc91e~mv2.jpg) described the undertaking as a $400 million project six years in the making. The [year-end accounts, page 25](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=25), recorded a more specific measure: about $282 million in completed facilities at opening, covering the park and recreation facilities, transportation, utilities, roads, and service buildings. The report discussed the hotel purchase and further construction [separately](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=31). The company's broad project description and its completed-facilities accounting subtotal measured different things.

## Building a functioning resort

The [1969 resort plan](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_6a193a0df52c464e8906206bd932f43f.pdf#page=2) connected hotels, lakes, transport, and the theme park across the property. Making that arrangement usable required extensive work below and between the attractions. Disney's [1968 report, page 7](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1968/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201968.pdf?download=1#page=9) recorded more than 1.5 million cubic yards of earth moved that year to form lagoons and channels and raise ground, including the future theme-park site.

By 1970, a four-lane parkway connected the main entrance with the vacation complex, and Florida had completed two major access interchanges. Service corridors beneath the theme park were being developed for utilities, waste handling, and deliveries. The nearly completed Central Energy Plant was designed to recover heat from its generators for heating and cooling. The company's [1970 construction account, page 12](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1970/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201970.pdf?download=1#page=14) shows why a resort of this scale needed much of the machinery of a small town before it could receive vacationers.

Construction brought additional companies into both the work and its financing. The [original arrangement](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=31) called for U.S. Steel's realty division to own the Contemporary and Polynesian hotels and lease them to a Disney subsidiary. Disney's [1970 construction report, page 13](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1970/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201970.pdf?download=1#page=15) identified WED as master planner and Welton Becket and Associates as architect. Steel guest rooms were assembled in a plant on the property, carried by truck to the hotels, then hoisted into position and connected to utilities.

Disney also expanded its own construction organization. In [December 1970, Donn Tatum reported](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1970/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201970.pdf?download=1#page=25) that Buena Vista Construction had assumed the general contractor's responsibilities at Walt Disney World.

*The resort taking shape in September 1971, shortly before its public opening. The Magic Kingdom, lakes, and Contemporary Hotel appear in one view. [State Archives of Florida, C678614](https://floridamemory.com/items/show/86540). Public domain.*

## What opened in 1971

Walt Disney World opened to the public on [October 1, 1971](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=31). Roy dedicated it on [October 25](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=3). In his [signed message](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=4), Roy attributed spending above earlier estimates to inflation and an expansion of the work.

Magic Kingdom was the theme park within the larger vacation resort. The first season's facilities included the Contemporary and Polynesian Village hotels, the Palm and Magnolia golf courses, beaches and boating, and a network of monorails, steamboats, and motor trams, described in Disney's [Winter/Spring 1972 Vacationland, pages 4-5, 17, and 19](https://www.disneydocs.net/_files/ugd/5db718_d60e4bbe81a74d098d742c76b0c644d8.pdf#page=4). By his [December 9 shareholder letter](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=4), Roy was also reporting operating campgrounds. A vacation could extend beyond a day inside the park.

For a visitor, the completed facilities came with choices and prices. A surviving October 1971 adult ticket book offered Magic Kingdom admission, eleven attraction coupons, and a transportation ticket for $5.75, including 23 cents in state tax. Its instructions allowed unlimited use that day and night of monorails, steamboats, and motor trams. The [original cover](https://mousemuseum.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/img150.jpg) and [transportation terms](https://mousemuseum.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/img151.jpg) show part of what a guest was buying. Lodging remained a separate purchase. Within the park, the attraction coupons helped determine how the visitor could spend the day.

![Visitors walk along a ramp beneath the elevated Walt Disney World monorail in October 1971.](oip-image:essays/walt-disney-world-opening-1971/visitor-transport)

*Visitors and the monorail in October 1971: transportation had become part of the resort experience. [State Archives of Florida, C678601](https://floridamemory.com/items/show/86529). Public domain.*

Financial arrangements continued after the gates opened. In December, Disney [agreed to buy U.S. Steel's hotel interests](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=31) for approximately $50 million plus completion costs. The same year's report also described [Lake Buena Vista residential development and further EPCOT plans](https://archive.org/download/wdp-annual-report-1971/WDP%20Annual%20Report%201971.pdf?download=1#page=12). Lake Buena Vista was a separate community development on the northeastern part of the property. Disney presented it as a way to gain real-estate experience and introduce ideas for the future EPCOT. The operating vacation resort was a substantial first phase of the Florida undertaking.

Walt's proposals had set out a place where the company could shape more of a visitor's stay, and eventually experiment with a community beyond it. Roy's task was to keep development within reach of an operating business: arrange capital, divide responsibility, and carry construction through to paying guests. By October 1971, visitors could board the boats and walk the streets that designers had put on paper. The audience could now walk into the picture.
