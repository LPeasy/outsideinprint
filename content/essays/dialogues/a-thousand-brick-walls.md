The first note came out wrong.

A clean guitar chord was supposed to open the show. Instead, feedback burst from a floor monitor and sent several hundred people reaching for their ears.

The guitarist lifted both hands from the strings.

The drummer laughed.

Someone near the stage yelled, “Turn it up!”

The Bellwether Theatre had survived ninety years, three owners, one fire, twelve years without heat, and a restoration that had stopped just short of making the place look new. Gold paint clung to the ceiling in thin islands. Red velvet covered the seats. The side walls had been stripped down to their original brick.

The theater looked better because some damage remained.

Syd stood beneath the balcony with a plastic cup of beer in his hand. Oliver stood beside him, watching a sound technician crawl behind an amplifier.

**Oliver:** They weren’t ready.

**Syd:** They started.

**Oliver:** That is not the same thing.

The drummer tapped his sticks together. The singer spoke into her microphone.

Nothing came out.

Syd smiled.

**Syd:** The whole point of doing this is to start doing it.

**Oliver:** The microphone is off.

**Syd:** It can be turned on.

**Oliver:** It should have been turned on before they walked out.

The singer tried again.

“Good evening,” she said.

This time her voice came through the speakers twice as loud as it needed to. The crowd cheered anyway.

Oliver folded his arms.

**Syd:** There. Problem solved.

**Oliver:** That was not a solution. Someone moved a knob.

**Syd:** Most solutions are someone moving a knob.

The band started again. The guitarist played the opening chord. It was too loud, but it was a chord.

Syd raised his cup.

**Syd:** It’s not gonna be right. It’s never gonna be right because it could always be better.

**Oliver:** That is the sort of thing people say after they forget to test the microphone.

**Syd:** The microphone being on would help.

**Oliver:** Thank you.

**Syd:** But it doesn’t make starting wrong.

Oliver watched the technician hurry across the back of the stage with a cable coiled around his arm.

**Oliver:** If you’re laying a brick wall, how you start is crucial to the whole project.

Syd turned toward the brick beside them.

**Syd:** A life is a thousand brick walls. We’ll be building them as long as we’re here.

**Oliver:** And each one has to stand.

**Syd:** Long enough to get you to the next one.

**Oliver:** You are trying to turn one wall into a thousand so you can excuse the crooked one.

**Syd:** I’m trying to say no beginning carries everything.

**Oliver:** This one is a brick wall, in my opinion.

**Syd:** Why?

**Oliver:** I like brick walls.

Syd looked at him for a moment.

**Syd:** That’s your argument?

**Oliver:** It’s a strong material.

**Syd:** That’s not what I asked.

**Oliver:** It lasts.

**Syd:** So do bad ideas.

**Oliver:** Not without maintenance.

The opening band pushed through the first song. The singer missed an entrance. The bass rattled the glasses behind the bar. The guitarist kept looking toward the sound booth, but by the second chorus the room had begun to settle around them.

A few people moved closer to the stage.

A woman near the aisle started dancing.

By the end of the song, the crowd gave the band a real cheer.

Syd nodded toward the stage.

**Syd:** You see?

**Oliver:** I see a band that should have done another sound check.

**Syd:** You see that they recovered.

**Oliver:** They recovered because the drummer held time, the singer did not panic, and someone behind the stage knew which cable was bad.

**Syd:** So preparation matters.

**Oliver:** Yes.

**Syd:** But the only reason any of that mattered is because they started.

Oliver glanced at him.

**Oliver:** You think everything is a race against hesitation.

**Syd:** And you think hesitation becomes wisdom if you give it a checklist.

**Oliver:** That is not what I think.

**Syd:** It sounds like what you think.

**Oliver:** You always do that.

**Syd:** Do what?

**Oliver:** Ask me what I think and then answer for me.

**Syd:** I’m trying to understand your attitude.

**Oliver:** No. You’re naming my attitude and handing it back to me.

The band moved into its second song. The sound was better now. The singer’s voice rose cleanly over the room.

Syd looked toward the stage.

**Syd:** I don’t think either of us is completely right or wrong.

**Oliver:** That is easy to say after assigning me a position I don’t hold.

**Syd:** That’s fair.

Oliver waited.

**Syd:** What I should have said is, “What do you mean by ‘do it right’?”

**Oliver:** I mean the audience paid to be here. The crew showed up. The band asked for the stage. They owe those people effort.

**Syd:** Not perfection?

**Oliver:** No.

**Syd:** Readiness?

**Oliver:** As much readiness as the work allows.

**Syd:** That sounds different.

**Oliver:** It is what I meant.

**Syd:** It isn’t what I heard.

**Oliver:** You heard the word *right* and built a prison around it.

**Syd:** You heard the word *start* and imagined a man throwing bricks from a moving truck.

Oliver almost smiled.

**Oliver:** That would be a poor way to build a wall.

**Syd:** But he’d have started.

**Oliver:** He would have made a pile.

**Syd:** Every wall starts as a pile.

**Oliver:** No. Every wall starts as a plan.

**Syd:** Every plan starts as a thought.

**Oliver:** Every thought starts as what?

**Syd:** I don’t know. Hunger. Fear. Boredom. God.

**Oliver:** So we have arrived nowhere.

**Syd:** We arrived at the beginning.

The second song ended without incident.

The singer thanked the crowd and introduced the band. They had driven six hours that morning. It was their first time playing in a theater that large.

Oliver looked at Syd.

**Oliver:** Does that change your view?

**Syd:** It makes me like them more.

**Oliver:** It makes the lack of preparation more understandable.

**Syd:** See?

**Oliver:** Understandable is not the same as good.

**Syd:** Nothing is ever good enough for you.

**Oliver:** That is not true.

**Syd:** You just changed *right* to *good*.

**Oliver:** You changed *understandable* to *correct*.

They stared at each other.

The next song started.

For several seconds, neither spoke.

A beam of blue light crossed the theater and caught the dust above the crowd. The old ceiling seemed to float there, held up by darkness.

Syd leaned closer so he could be heard.

**Syd:** I like that we can get this wrong and keep talking.

**Oliver:** You enjoy starting trouble and calling the repair interesting.

**Syd:** I enjoy that neither of us has to pretend agreement to stay in the room.

**Oliver:** Some people call that courtesy.

**Syd:** Some people call silence courtesy and resentment peace.

**Oliver:** Then ask before you accuse.

**Syd:** I’m practicing recovery.

**Oliver:** After practicing accusation.

The opening band finished its set.

This time, several rows stood.

The singer leaned into the microphone and thanked the sound crew. The technician beside the stage gave a small wave without looking up from his cables.

The house lights came on.

People moved toward the lobby in a slow press of coats, drinks, and bodies. Syd and Oliver followed them beneath the balcony and into a narrow hall lined with framed posters from old productions.

A brass plate near the stairs showed the theater before its restoration. Half the windows had been boarded over. Weeds grew through cracks in the sidewalk. Several letters were missing from the marquee.

Oliver stopped beside a section of exposed wall.

The bricks were not all the same. Some were dark and smooth. Others were pale, rough, and newer. Mortar ran between them in lines of different widths.

Syd touched one of the darker bricks.

**Syd:** Which one was first?

**Oliver:** It is probably behind something.

**Syd:** Exactly.

**Oliver:** That does not mean it did not matter.

**Syd:** It means nobody knows which one it was.

**Oliver:** The wall knows.

**Syd:** The wall doesn’t know anything.

**Oliver:** Then why are you talking to it?

Syd removed his hand.

**Syd:** You keep treating every beginning like it has to carry the whole future.

**Oliver:** You keep treating every beginning like it carries nothing.

**Syd:** It carries enough to get you to the next one.

**Oliver:** Sometimes the next one only exists because the first one held.

**Syd:** And sometimes the first one falls down.

**Oliver:** Then you build it again.

Syd looked at him.

**Syd:** That sounds like my argument.

**Oliver:** No. Your argument is that it does not matter how you begin.

**Syd:** It matters less than beginning.

**Oliver:** That is different.

**Syd:** It’s a big difference.

**Oliver:** It is also wrong.

**Syd:** Why?

**Oliver:** Because beginning badly can cost more than waiting.

**Syd:** Waiting can cost everything.

**Oliver:** Yes.

**Syd:** So neither rule works every time.

**Oliver:** No.

**Syd:** Then why are we arguing?

**Oliver:** Because you keep trying to turn judgment into a rule.

**Syd:** I thought you were the one with the rule.

**Oliver:** My rule is to think before doing something.

**Syd:** That’s not a rule. That’s a slogan.

**Oliver:** It is better than “Do something.”

**Syd:** Nothing has ever been made by someone who only thought about making it.

**Oliver:** Nothing worth keeping has ever been made by someone who refused to think.

A bell rang inside the theater.

The headliner would begin in five minutes.

The lobby started to empty.

Neither of them moved.

Syd looked again at the wall.

**Syd:** When I ask why, I’m not trying to catch you.

**Oliver:** It can sound that way.

**Syd:** I ask because I think your answer is worth hearing.

**Oliver:** Then leave room for me to give it.

**Syd:** I’m working on the delivery.

**Oliver:** That is preparation.

Syd laughed.

**Syd:** There it is.

**Oliver:** There what is?

**Syd:** The whole argument.

**Oliver:** It is not the whole argument.

**Syd:** It’s close.

They walked back into the theater.

Stagehands had cleared the opening band’s equipment. A single microphone stood at center stage. Behind it, the red curtain had been lifted to reveal a bare brick wall at the back of the stage.

The crowd returned to its seats.

Syd and Oliver stood where they had been before.

**Syd:** This only works because I assume you mean what you say.

**Oliver:** And because I assume you might listen.

**Syd:** Good faith.

**Oliver:** Good faith does not fix the microphone.

**Syd:** No.

**Oliver:** It does not make a bad argument true.

**Syd:** No.

**Oliver:** It does not make a bad beginning harmless.

**Syd:** No.

Oliver turned to him.

**Oliver:** Then what does it do?

**Syd:** It keeps us talking while someone fixes the microphone.

The lights went down.

The crowd rose with a roar.

A woman walked onto the stage carrying an old wooden guitar. She stepped to the microphone and said something no one could hear.

The microphone was off.

She looked toward the sound booth.

The crowd laughed.

She laughed too.

A technician raised one finger.

The theater went quiet.

The woman waited.

Then the microphone came alive.

She leaned toward it.

“Let’s try that again,” she said.

The crowd cheered louder than before.

She placed her fingers on the strings.

Oliver looked at Syd.

**Oliver:** The first brick matters.

Syd nodded.

**Syd:** One of a thousand.

The first chord filled the theater.
