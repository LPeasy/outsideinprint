*Beyond their boat, lights picked out a long hull against the dark water. A woman pointed to a place where she thought the deck had been a moment before. The man beside her leaned forward.*

*Now they could see it. The water climbed, the long outline tilted, and the last visible part slipped beneath the surface.*

*For a moment, nobody at the rail spoke.*

*Then someone began to clap. Others joined in. A man smiled and put his phone away. Beside him, a woman shook her head.*

*They had come to watch the SS United States become a reef.*

*An imagined scene at the sinking.*

---

The sinking took place on [November 11, 2026, off Destin, Florida](https://6abc.com/live-updates/ss-united-states-live-stream-historic-vessel-sunk-become-worlds-largest-artificial-reef-off-florida-coast/20033077/). Crews let water into the hull, and the work went on into the night. Okaloosa County, on Florida's Gulf Coast, bought the ship to create what it called [the world's largest artificial reef](https://myokaloosa.com/SSUSFAQs). The county hoped the wreck would give sea life a place to grow and draw divers and fishing boats.

## A liner with a second job

The original contract dated to [April 1949](https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/public-papers/177/letter-attorney-general-sale-ss-united-states). United States Lines wanted a new passenger liner, and the federal government helped pay for it. A strong fleet of American merchant ships served trade in peacetime. This ship could also [carry troops if another war required it](https://catalogs.marinersmuseum.org/object/CL20552).

Ship designer William Francis Gibbs spent decades trying to build a fast American superliner. At Newport News, Virginia, builders followed his firm's plans for [a steel hull with a lighter aluminum *superstructure*](https://www.ssusc.org/history-design-launch), the part built above it. Inside, separate compartments helped keep a leak from flooding the whole ship. If damage disabled one of the two engine rooms, the other could keep her moving. Her speed would serve paying passengers in peace and troop movements in war.

Workers laid the keel, the long base of the hull, on February 8, 1950. The liner measured 990 feet long and was [launched on June 23, 1951](https://catalogs.marinersmuseum.org/object/CL20552).

Before the first passenger voyage, Americans were already arguing about the bill. In June 1952, President Harry Truman put the government's cost of building the ship at [almost $78 million](https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/public-papers/177/letter-attorney-general-sale-ss-united-states). Under the original contract, United States Lines would pay about $28 million. Truman backed the ship's trade and defense roles, but thought the company might be getting too much help from taxpayers. He asked the attorney general what could be done about the subsidy.

## A fast crossing, with time for deck games

The United States left New York on her first passenger voyage on July 3, 1952. From Ambrose lightship near New York to Bishop Rock off England, she crossed in [three days, ten hours and forty minutes](https://catalogs.marinersmuseum.org/object/CL20552). That was the timed part of the voyage, with an average speed of 35.59 knots. A *[knot](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/nautical-mile-knot.html)* is one nautical mile per hour. The return trip also set a record, and the ship took the Blue Riband, the famous honor for speed among Atlantic passenger liners.

An *ocean liner* carried people between ports on a set schedule. Passengers might enjoy a grand trip, but they also had somewhere to go. A few days at sea were part of getting to Europe, or getting home. Over time, more people bought cruises for the voyage itself, a change we explored in [The Making of the Floating Palace](https://outsideinprint.org/essays/the-making-of-the-floating-palace/).

Inside, [designer Dorothy Marckwald](https://www.ssusc.org/history-the-glory-years) had to make the rooms inviting while meeting Gibbs' strict rules on fire safety. She worked with bright colors and glass artwork in rooms that largely avoided wood. The grand pianos and the kitchen's butcher blocks got an exception.

Even the [shuffleboard pucks were plastic](https://www.ssusc.org/the-ship). On the sports deck, passengers played that game or tossed a rubber disk across a net in deck tennis. Wind deflectors helped shield them as the ship sped along. There was time for a game before dinner, even aboard the fastest liner on the Atlantic.

In a memoir excerpt, [Douglas Shepardson recalled a childhood crossing](https://www.ssusc.org/news/2021/02/doug-shepardson-life-in-my-time) after a family tour of Europe. His parents let the children roam. He played tag on the promenade, swam on C deck and went to the movies. At breakfast in the first-class dining room, he ordered blueberries and cream instead of his usual cereal. When he asked for another bowl, his mother questioned whether one was enough. The waiter brought it anyway.

## When travelers took to the air

In 1958, [airlines began flying jets across the Atlantic](https://www.ssusc.org/history-the-glory-years). Passengers could now make the trip in hours. The United States could fill up in summer, but speed at sea wasn't enough to keep the business sound. The company faced disputes with workers, the cost of running the ship and yearly arguments over federal aid.

In November 1969, while the ship was at Newport News for her yearly overhaul, United States Lines [took her out of service](https://www.ssusc.org/history-the-glory-years). Her passenger career lasted about seventeen years. According to the SS United States Conservancy, the group later formed to preserve her, some crew members [left belongings aboard expecting to return to work](https://www.ssusc.org/history-retirement). They came back to find the gangway doors closed.

The ship stayed in reserve until 1978. A plan for seagoing timeshare homes failed, and her [interior fittings went to auction in 1984](https://www.ssusc.org/history-retirement). A later owner sent her to Turkey and Ukraine to remove harmful materials. She reached Philadelphia in 1996.

A [Norwegian Cruise Line plan to restore service](https://www.ssusc.org/history-retirement) also failed. With financial help from H. F. Lenfest, the Conservancy [became her owner in February 2011](https://www.ssusc.org/our-history). The group hoped to preserve her, but needed a use that could pay the bills.

## The cost of waiting

[Hotel rooms, restaurants and exhibits](https://www.ssusc.org/our-history) all appeared in plans for the ship. Each proposal needed money for the work and a place willing to take her. In the meantime, the bills for keeping her afloat kept coming. The Conservancy continued its search, but never secured a permanent home.

On June 14, 2024, a federal court put [a deadline on the stay in Philadelphia](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-paed-2_22-cv-02285/pdf/USCOURTS-paed-2_22-cv-02285-0.pdf). The landlord tried to double the daily fee, and the Conservancy refused to pay the increase. The court found that the refusal didn't break their agreement. But the agreement had no fixed end date, and the landlord could end it with notice. The ship would have to leave. What to do with her next was up to her owners.

In October 2024, Okaloosa County became the owner. After years of failed plans, the Conservancy said a reef and a museum on land were [the last workable choice other than scrapping her](https://www.ssusc.org/frequently-asked-questions). The county saw a place where sea life could grow and visitors could come to [dive and fish](https://myokaloosa.com/SSUSFAQs).

The United States spent far longer waiting for another job than carrying passengers. In February 2025, she [left Philadelphia under tow](https://www.ssusc.org/frequently-asked-questions) and [reached Mobile, Alabama, on March 3](https://myokaloosa.com/sites/default/files/Users/piouser/Updated-SSUS-120925.pdf). Workers there began making her ready to sink.

## Making the ship ready to sink

Fuel and oil can remain in tanks, while wiring, insulation and paint can hold harmful substances. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) gives [guidance on dealing with these materials](https://www.epa.gov/marine-protection-permitting/vessel-reef-projects) before a ship becomes a reef. It covers oil, asbestos and *[PCBs](https://www.epa.gov/pcbs/learn-about-polychlorinated-biphenyls)*, a group of harmful industrial chemicals, among other substances.

According to the [county's cleanup report](https://www.destinfwb.com/explore/eco-tourism/ssus/ssus-remediation-updates/), workers emptied and cleaned all 128 fuel and lubricant tanks, which a marine chemist certified clean. They removed loose exterior paint and wiring, along with flooring that contained high levels of PCBs. More than 200 material samples were collected during the work.

Her [two funnels, radar mast and a propeller](https://www.destinfwb.com/explore/eco-tourism/ssus/ssus-remediation-updates/) were set aside for the planned museum. Hundreds of historic objects and more than 400 portholes were kept too. Some of the ship would be available for people who preferred to visit on dry land.

A poor weather forecast led the county to move the planned November 12 sinking [ahead by one day](https://business.destinchamber.com/news/details/ss-united-states-artificial-reef-deployment-date-moved-up-to-nov-11). On November 11, the work went on past daylight until [the sea closed over the hull](https://6abc.com/live-updates/ss-united-states-live-stream-historic-vessel-sunk-become-worlds-largest-artificial-reef-off-florida-coast/20033077/).

## What else could the metal have become?

A sunken ship gives sea life surfaces to grow on and fish places to hide. A reef may help [more fish survive and grow](https://sanctuaries.noaa.gov/science/conservation/pdfs/artificial_reef.pdf), draw them from nearby waters, or do both. The result depends on the site, the kinds of fish and how heavily people fish there. Finding fish around a wreck is a start. Learning whether it helped the wider fish population takes more study.

The county [reported a $13 million allocation](https://myokaloosa.com/SSUSFAQs) for buying, towing and cleaning the ship and helping fund a museum on land. That figure was a budget allocation, not a final spending total. It said [tourism revenue and partner contributions funded the project](https://business.destinchamber.com/news/details/ss-united-states-artificial-reef-deployment-date-moved-up-to-nov-11), including a tax on short-term rentals. The museum would preserve the ship's history. Dive and fishing operators could earn money from visits to the wreck. Benefits to marine life would need to be measured in the water.

The ship's metal offered another use. Most ship recycling takes place in [South Asia and Türkiye](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch2_en.pdf). Retired cruise ships go to these yards too. In 2020, Carnival sent the Fantasy and Inspiration to Turkish yards in [a recycling program managed with Sea2Cradle](https://www.sea2cradle.com/new-collaboration-between-carnival-corporation-and-sea2cradle-on-responsible-recycling-of-cruise-vessels/).

Taking a ship apart puts steel and other useful materials back to work. It also puts workers among [heavy parts and harmful substances](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/the-hong-kong-international-convention-for-the-safe-and-environmentally-sound-recycling-of-ships.aspx). A yard's practices can determine how much of the gain from recycling comes at the expense of those workers and the coast around them.

There's an energy saving to consider too. The International Energy Agency estimates that making steel from scrap in an *electric arc furnace*, which uses electric heat, takes [60 to 70 percent less energy](https://www.iea.org/articles/driving-energy-efficiency-in-heavy-industries) than making it from iron ore. Even federal guidance for reef projects calls for [saving useful equipment and metals during preparation](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-09/documents/artificialreefguidance.pdf).

*Two possible afterlives. Reefing can provide habitat and diving opportunities. Recycling returns metals to use. Both require cleanup and a careful accounting of their wider costs.*

Was putting the United States underwater the best use of her metal? We'd need to compare towing and cleanup, the cost of taking her apart, the value of recovered materials and the habitat a reef could provide. Materials already saved for reuse or the museum belong in that account too. The records used for this story don't make that full environmental comparison.

A team led by scientists at Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution plans to [track the life that settles on the ship](https://www.whoi.edu/press-room/news-release/ssus-reef/), changes in the water's chemistry and what happens as the metal corrodes. Their work starts soon after the sinking and continues through later visits. Those findings will help assess the reef's effects. A comparison with recycling would also need to count the materials and energy that option could have saved.

In 1952, a clock and two points on the Atlantic measured the United States' achievement. Her final assignment will take years to measure.
