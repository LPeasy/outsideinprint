My journey to work begins on a hill.

I live in Itaewon, a Seoul neighborhood known for its restaurants and nightlife. On weekday mornings, I walk past houses, walls, and trees on a steep side street. I head downhill to catch a bus toward Yeoksam, where I work in architecture, construction, and interiors.

![Tiled rooftops and a tree above a hillside neighborhood](oip-image:essays/between-buses-in-seoul/itaewon-rooftops)

*Rooftops along the walk to a bus stop in Itaewon. Photo by Tristan Kim / PG Global Insight.*

For me, the remarkable thing about this ordinary trip is the space between buildings. It's where I cross the road, change buses, and wait for the next one. Some of the choices Seoul has made about those spaces change how traffic moves. Others change what a journey costs or how the same few minutes feel.

## A bus stop in the middle of the road

On some of Seoul's major streets, you cross to a raised island to catch the bus. From the island you can see traffic flowing on both sides, while buses have a lane of their own.

![Blue buses line one side of a narrow brick bus platform while a car and a white coach pass on the other.](oip-image:essays/between-buses-in-seoul/section-2)

*Buses pull up beside a median platform in Seoul while traffic passes on the other side. Photo by Tristan Kim / PG Global Insight.*

Seoul calls these *median bus-only lanes* and [keeps them in force around the clock](https://news.seoul.go.kr/traffic/archives/35252). Buses and [certain other authorized vehicles](https://news.seoul.go.kr/traffic/archives/35276) can use them. Drivers of ordinary cars use the other lanes. Buses can get held up at lights or at busy stops.

Giving buses priority sets aside space that could go to other traffic. Moving the stop to the middle of the road gives the city another job. A faster bus only helps if you can cross to the stop safely. That takes a crossing people can see and reach, clear signals, and enough room on the platform to wait.

## Changing buses near Nonhyeon

I don't always have a direct bus from Itaewon to Yeoksam. One morning I changed buses near Nonhyeon Station. I could see the Mega Coffee sign from where I stood waiting to go on to work.

![View from a brick-paved strip between traffic lanes, with office towers on both sides and a Mega Coffee sign at upper right.](oip-image:essays/between-buses-in-seoul/nonhyeon-stop)

*The street near Nonhyeon Station where Kim waited to change buses. Photo by Tristan Kim / PG Global Insight.*

When I tap the same transit card as I get on and off each ride, Seoul can count those rides as one trip. On routes that take part, I can change buses or switch between the bus and subway without paying a second full base fare. [The fare starts with the highest base fare among the services I use](https://news.seoul.go.kr/traffic/traffic_price). A longer trip can cost more.

Under [Seoul's transfer rules](https://news.seoul.go.kr/traffic/transfer_discount), I usually have 30 minutes after getting off to catch the next ride. If I board the next ride between 9 p.m. and 7 a.m., the window is 60 minutes. The discount covers up to four changes across five rides in a row. It doesn't apply if I get back on the same bus or take another bus on the same route.

The buses and subway run on different routes, but their fare rules work together. Good city design can be an operational rule as much as a physical object.

## Five minutes near Yeoksam

Near Yeoksam I photographed an enclosed bus shelter with a bench, broad windows, wood-toned surfaces, and a digital display. Outside were traffic and office lights ~ inside was a small place to pause.

![An enclosed shelter with wide windows, a bench, and wood-toned finishes](oip-image:essays/between-buses-in-seoul/yeoksam-shelter)

*Inside the enclosed waiting area near Yeoksam. Photo by Tristan Kim / PG Global Insight.*

In Seoul, riders can check [estimated arrival times on their phones or on displays at some bus stops](https://english.seoul.go.kr/service/movement/public-transportation/).

![Digital board above an Itaewon bus shelter showing estimated waits of five and ten minutes.](oip-image:essays/between-buses-in-seoul/itaewon-arrival-display)

*An arrival display in Itaewon shows buses due in five and ten minutes. Photo by Tristan Kim / PG Global Insight.*

Five minutes on a bench with an arrival time in view feels different to me from five minutes standing in the cold, wondering when the bus will come.

In construction, we look at the size of a space, the materials we build it from, how safe it is, and what it takes to keep it working. The shelter needs cleaning and repairs. The screen needs electricity and reliable information. I don't know the full costs or performance figures for the particular stops I use.

At the end of the day, I look out through the shelter windows at the traffic near Yeoksam. It's a view shared by many people trying to get home after work. I have a place to sit and some idea of when I'll be on my way.
