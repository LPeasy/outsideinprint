Turn a clock back in November and the evening seems to disappear. Yesterday's five o'clock sunset becomes today's four o'clock sunset. The morning gains the same hour on the clock. The amount of daylight has barely changed ~ the labels have.

We want daylight after work, but we also need to get up, travel and send children to school in the morning. Under *daylight saving time*, clocks run an hour ahead of *standard time*. Turning them back puts that hour of light back in the morning. In the United States, federal law sets the seasonal schedule where daylight saving is observed. To end the changes nationwide, we'd have to choose between more evening light and earlier winter sunrises.

The clock change shifts the workday relative to the sun if work and school keep the same start and finish times. Your shift can begin at nine on either clock. When the clocks move forward, you arrive an hour earlier by the sun and leave an hour earlier, too, with more daylight after work. Changing clocks together keeps workplaces, trains and customers on a shared schedule. One person getting up early can't make the rest of the town open early. The [1916 British debate](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1916/may/08/daylight-saving) made that case.

Benjamin Franklin's [1784 satire](https://www.webexhibits.org/daylightsaving/franklin3.html) urged late risers in Paris to save candles by getting up earlier. He didn't propose resetting clocks. New Zealand's George Vernon Hudson [proposed seasonal clock changes in 1895](https://paperspast.natlib.govt.nz/periodicals/TPRSNZ1898-31.2.6.1.58). William Willett later [campaigned for them in Britain](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~gent0113/wettijd/downloads/willet_1914.pdf).

[Writing to The Spectator in 1907](https://archive.spectator.co.uk/article/14th-september-1907/17/to-the-editor-of-the-spectator-sinthe-letter-of-mr), W. A. Fox described a city clerk with little chance to get outdoors before work. He also described a shopkeeper stuck at his counter all day. More of the daylight would fall after the clerk finished work and the shopkeeper closed his shop.

During World War I, advocates made a different case for the same clock change: using less fuel. In [Britain's 1916 parliamentary debate](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1916/may/08/daylight-saving), supporters argued that using lights for fewer hours would save coal. Their case included both gas and electric light. America's Fuel Administrator, H. A. Garfield, also [predicted substantial coal savings](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1918-pt4-v56/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1918-pt4-v56-8.pdf). Congress [adopted the federal rule in March 1918](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-40/pdf/STATUTE-40-Pg451.pdf).

The first federal rule lasted through two summers. In 1919, the National Grange and other farm groups [demanded repeal](https://www.congress.gov/66/crecb/1919/07/18/GPO-CRECB-1919-pt3-v58-11-2.pdf). A repeal supporter [argued that dairy farmers had to start an hour earlier to meet train schedules](https://www.congress.gov/66/crecb/1919/07/18/GPO-CRECB-1919-pt3-v58-11-2.pdf#page=18). President Woodrow Wilson acknowledged the problems for farmers, but he [defended its value to manufacturers and merchants](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/message-the-house-representatives-returning-without-approval-act-for-the-repeal-the). Congress overrode his veto and [repealed the seasonal provision in 1919](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-41/pdf/STATUTE-41-Pg280.pdf). Calling daylight saving a favor to farmers gets the history backward.

[Year-round daylight time began during World War II](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-56/pdf/STATUTE-56-Pg9.pdf), then [ended in 1945](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-59/pdf/STATUTE-59-Pg537.pdf). After the war, local rules differed. A train or bus could connect places that didn't change their clocks on the same schedule. Its timetable had to make sense at both ends of the journey.

In 1966, Representative John Jarman [described the expense and wasted staff time](https://www.congress.gov/89/crecb/1966/03/16/GPO-CRECB-1966-pt5-4-1.pdf) that different local clocks caused for railroads, buses and airlines. Congress passed the [Uniform Time Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-80/pdf/STATUTE-80-Pg107.pdf), which set common spring and autumn change dates while letting states keep standard time all year. The seasonal switch could now continue without another crisis or a vote each year. The carriers could have planned around a shared year-round schedule, too.

During the oil crisis, Congress tried winter daylight saving again. An [emergency law advanced clocks in January 1974](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-87/pdf/STATUTE-87-Pg707.pdf). Keeping summer's clock meant waiting longer for sunrise on school mornings. In October, President Gerald Ford [signed an amendment restoring standard time for the coming winter interval](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/library/document/0248/whpr19741005-002.pdf).

At the following year's Senate hearing, Senator John Stennis questioned the evidence for winter energy savings. He [urged Congress to return to the schedule people already knew](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-94shrg64545/pdf/CHRG-94shrg64545.pdf), making familiarity part of his case against winter daylight saving.

Congress later kept daylight saving for more of the year, with more than fuel in mind. Its [1986 legislation](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-100/pdf/STATUTE-100-Pg764.pdf) cited outdoor recreation, children's playtime, shopping hours and business coordination, as well as energy savings. The [2005 Energy Policy Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-109publ58/pdf/PLAW-109publ58.pdf) extended the season again. It produced the March-to-November schedule used since 2007.

In 2008, the Department of Energy estimated that the added weeks saved [0.03 percent of annual U.S. electricity consumption](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/office_eere/pdfs/epact_sec_110_edst_report_to_congress_2008.pdf). That study measured the added weeks, not the whole daylight-saving season. A separate study of Indiana's switch estimated [roughly 1 percent higher residential electricity use](https://haas.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/csemwp179.pdf). The extra heating and cooling used more electricity than the lights saved.

The studies covered different changes, customers and places. These findings don't support a promise of lower bills for everyone who changes clocks. The wartime advocates were already talking about electric light, too.

[Federal law](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/260a) lets states keep standard time all year. It doesn't let them choose permanent daylight time on their own, so states that want that schedule need Congress to change the law. Either permanent schedule would let us stop changing the clocks twice a year. We'd have to choose which one to keep, and the energy figures don't give us a single answer. Lawmakers have also weighed evening play and shopping against later winter sunrises.

The Senate [passed a permanent-daylight-time bill in 2022](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CREC-2022-03-15/html/CREC-2022-03-15-pt1-PgS1165-5.htm). In July 2026, the House [passed another, 308-117](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes/2026238), which was [referred to a Senate committee](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/BILLS-119hr139rfs/html/BILLS-119hr139rfs.htm). Those votes haven't changed the federal rule requiring the seasonal switch.

Keep daylight time through winter, and sunrise comes an hour later by the clock. Keep standard time through summer, and sunset comes an hour earlier. We can stop moving the hands. We can't give that same hour of daylight to both ends of the day.
