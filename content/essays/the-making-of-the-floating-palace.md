*Nassau, Bahamas · November 5, 2026*

[Nassau&#8217;s cruise schedule](https://nassaucruiseport.com/wp-content/themes/mbcglobalports/includes/api/schedule-list.php?site_id=30&param2=2026-11-05&param4=2026-11-05) for Thursday, November 5, 2026, reads like a catalogue of ways to get away. Among its six ships were four quite different versions of the holiday. The Revivalists advertised a four-night music festival from Miami aboard *Norwegian Jewel*. *Carnival Spirit* offered a water park for the kids. *Celebrity Reflection* offered half an acre of real grass on its upper deck. *Margaritaville at Sea Paradise* supplied a name that seemed almost redundant in the Bahamas. The day comes into view through its published schedules and sales pitches.[^1]

For an arriving passenger at Prince George Wharf, the island getaway could begin with a short walk across Nassau to catch another boat. At Elizabeth on Bay Marina, a local operator [advertised a three-hour excursion](https://pearlislandbahamas.com/experiences/swimming-pigs-express/) to swim with pigs. Its itinerary took visitors to the animals at Athol Island and included time at Pearl Island before the return to Nassau. Even the escape from the ship came with a schedule for getting back.[^2]

**How did we end up here? And why would anyone want to swim with pigs?**

A week away can bring a family rest, pleasure, and time together. Making that week possible takes years of work by shipbuilders, lenders, crews, and the governments that provide a place to dock. I want to follow those commitments back to the people who make them and ask what they accomplish. What does this industry give the communities it touches, and what could comparable care and ambition make possible in the places people call home?

## A passage for pleasure

In 1833, a party of travelers sailed from Naples aboard the steamship *Francesco I* on a pleasure excursion to Constantinople. One of them, a French traveler named Marchebeus, [published an account](https://archive.org/details/voyagedepariscon00marc) six years later. Near the front of his book comes a list of the people who went. Their names fit on two pages.

Marchebeus gives his own occupation as architect and his city as Paris. Above his name appear the hereditary prince of Bavaria, a Neapolitan ambassador, and a botanist. Beside some names he specifies where the travelers joined. A woman and her son took the passage from Smyrna to Constantinople. The Duchess of Berry and her party came aboard for the stretch from Palermo to Naples. Even on this early excursion, people made different journeys on the same ship.[^3]

The prince went to see his brother, King Otto of Greece. Marchebeus went for pleasure and instruction, and took a particular interest in ruins. What brought these purposes together was the Neapolitan steamship and the administration that advertised its voyage. On April 16 the sea off Naples was rough. The management wanted to keep time anyway, so the captain weighed anchor. Marchebeus recalls a crowd waving from the mole and three guns sounding the ship&#8217;s salute. The excursion ran four months. The ship&#8217;s journal, as Marchebeus summarized it, counted only 572 hours under way. Much of what people came for happened while the ship stood still.[^3]

In 1844, William Makepeace Thackeray went to dinner in London and came away with a Mediterranean itinerary. The Peninsular and Oriental Company proposed to get him to Malta, Athens, Constantinople, Jerusalem, and Cairo, and home by Lord Mayor&#8217;s Day. Thackeray worried about the cost until a friend suggested the company&#8217;s directors would give him a berth. He bought eighteen shirts and sailed from Southampton aboard the *Lady Mary Wood*. [His preface](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1863/pg1863-images.html) thanks the directors for the passage.[^4]

## A room that traveled

In February 1867, an American who wanted a berth could apply to 117 Wall Street. Charles C. [Duncan&#8217;s printed program](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3176/3176-h/3176-h.htm) offered an excursion to the Holy Land and a good many places on the way. The organizers chartered the side-wheel steamer *Quaker City*. An adult berth cost $1,250 in currency. Ten percent down secured a place once the committee approved the applicant. Early customers got their choice of rooms and seats at table. Having the money didn&#8217;t guarantee a berth.[^5]

The program treated the ship as a home which passengers could keep at any port. Anyone who preferred to stay aboard could do so at no extra charge. The ship covered the boat rides. For travelers who went farther afield, Duncan estimated five dollars a day in gold for expenses ashore. Even the ship&#8217;s route came with terms. The organizers could substitute a healthy port for one with contagious illness. Passengers could extend the trip or change the route if they all agreed. One holdout could keep everyone on schedule.[^5]

Mark Twain went, and two years later reproduced the program in *The Innocents Abroad*. The Reverend Henry Ward Beecher, whose presence lent the enterprise distinction, stayed home. Twain drily observed that the party could more willingly spare some of the people who actually sailed. Once at sea, the passengers put the purser on trial for stealing an overcoat from stateroom No. 10. It was a mock trial. They empaneled a jury, supplied lawyers for both sides, and made an evening&#8217;s entertainment out of the business. Duncan arranged the boat rides and the berths. Nobody at 117 Wall Street needed to invent the entertainment.[^5]

In 1881, [Hugh Wilkinson](https://archive.org/details/cu31924023562204) joined the *Ceylon* at Suez. He left the ship at Bombay to travel overland and rejoined it at Calcutta. His explanation for choosing a cruise will make sense to anybody who ever lost a suitcase. What he valued about the arrangement was the possibility of seeing places off the regular steamship routes while his luggage, his purchases, and a familiar bed traveled with him. A disappointing hotel ashore scarcely mattered if his own room lay in the harbor. He called it the comfort of knowing &#8220;our home is always near.&#8221;[^6]

Keeping that home comfortable gave other people work to do. [Christian Wilhelm Allers](https://www.cwallers.de/backschisch/backschisch_9-12.pdf), a German artist, brings a few of them into view. In January 1891 he carried his hand luggage through the snow at Cuxhaven to board the Hamburg-American Line&#8217;s *Augusta Victoria*. Musicians played. Stewards found places for passengers and bags. Allers negotiated a division of cabin space with his roommate, tested the electric light, and felt the bed. By the time he found his way back to the deck to wave goodbye, the ship was already moving.[^7]

The Hamburg shipping executive Albert Ballin saw an opportunity in the winter calendar. A fast liner cost money alongside the pier, and demand for North Atlantic crossings fell in the cold months. The *Augusta Victoria* could earn its keep taking passengers south. Ballin went along on the 1891 trip.[^7]

By February 1901, the Hamburg-American Line could show a reporter what happened when the holiday got a ship of its own. The *Prinzessin Victoria Luise* could make sixteen knots. [According to](https://www.survivorlibrary.com/library/scientific-american-1901-02-02-v84-n05.pdf#page=3) *Scientific American*, she would ordinarily cruise at thirteen to thirteen and a half. On a passage sold for speed, the extra time at sea might count against the company. A pleasure cruise gave the passenger reason to welcome a slower trip.[^8]

The reporter counted capacity by the seats in the dining saloon, enough for 220. All passengers traveled first class. Their cabins dispensed with upper berths, and many held a single bed. A passenger could turn a crank to swing open a porthole in warmer latitudes. Three-sided shelters faced aft so a passenger could sit outside in foul weather. There was a gymnasium and a darkroom. A traveler could expose a plate ashore and develop the photograph at sea. The shipyard found room for that particular pleasure on a vessel only 47 feet wide.[^8]

## The servants&#8217; fare

Canadian Pacific&#8217;s [fare sheet](https://digitize.library.ubc.ca/2022/10/11/world-tour-in-the-early-1930s-the-empress-of-britain-world-cruise/) for the 1931&#8211;32 world cruise of the *Empress of Britain* set out the accommodations by deck and room number. Two people could book a suite with a sitting room, sunroom, reception hall, and separate baggage room for $26,000. Farther down the sheet, a berth in a two-person room on F Deck cost $2,000 per person. Beneath the columns came the arrangements for anyone who wished to bring a servant.[^9]

Canadian Pacific charged $1,570 for a servant traveling with an employer. That bought separate quarters and meals in special accommodations. It included railway transportation on specified routes, among them Haifa to Jerusalem and Cairo to Suez. The servant&#8217;s fare excluded hotel rooms and sightseeing. The sheet specified a $2,000 fare for servants who joined the included excursions at all ports and went sightseeing with their employer or with other servants.[^9]

A ship&#8217;s steward and a passenger&#8217;s servant might each spend the morning looking after the same family. One drew pay from Canadian Pacific. The other appeared in its accounts as a fare. Both helped the family enjoy a holiday; the fare sheet priced the servant&#8217;s own access to its pleasures separately.

[Heinrich Hoffmann&#8217;s photograph](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/nazi-germany-1933-1945/strength-through-joy-cruise-to-madeira-1938) of a 1938 Kraft durch Freude voyage to Madeira shows a sun deck full of people. The Nazi regime&#8217;s Strength Through Joy organization, part of the German Labor Front, offered organized holidays to workers and their families. A crowded deck made good publicity for a regime that claimed to overcome class divisions.[^10]

War put liners to other uses. [Cunard White Star&#8217;s 1949 brochure](https://www.ggarchives.com/OT/Brochures/CunardWSL-1949-FirstClassToEurope.html) brought the *Britannic* back to the paying public after government service and a refit. Readers could inspect the swimming pool on C Deck and the cocktail lounge on the promenade deck. In the same brochure the company introduced its new *Caronia*, designed both for cruises and for service to Europe. You could buy a crossing for the practical purpose of getting to the other side. The line would happily sell you first-class pleasures on the way. A cruise let the line sell that time at sea to someone quite willing to finish where they started.[^11]

## A pier at either end

In 1958 about two-thirds of Americans traveling to Europe went by air, [the U.S. Commerce Department reported](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/survey-current-business-46/june-1959-9598/fulltext). The number who went by sea rose a little that year. A growing share of a growing business went to the airlines. A shipowner competing with an airplane on the way from New York to Europe faced a considerable disadvantage. A shipowner selling a week in the Caribbean could find the airplane an excellent business partner.[^12]

In [interviews published in Miami in January 1970](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1970-pt1/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1970-pt1-4-3.pdf#page=33), cruise operators described convincing a Northern vacationer to fly to Florida, board a ship, and start the warm part of the holiday there. Sailing from New York meant spending a couple of days getting to the warmth. Flying to Miami left more of a short vacation for the Caribbean and gave the port a way to attract passengers who might once have sailed from New York. Miami&#8217;s contribution to the bargain was a seaport that local taxpayers helped build.[^13]

In April 1960, Miami and Dade County agreed to build new seaport facilities on Dodge Island. The city supplied land, the existing port, and money it accumulated for construction. The county undertook to seek about $2 million a year in property taxes as work progressed. Taxpayers sued. In 1961 [a judge upheld the plan](https://hallapproved.com/fl/cases/miami-dade-county-circuit-court/1961/5066609/) and explained its limits. Each succeeding county commission could decide how much to appropriate. The port served cargo as well as passengers, and its revenue helped pay for the work. The cruise lines&#8217; departures depended on piers that county commissioners financed through annual budgets.[^14]

Dade County faced another transportation decision in those same years. It sought [authority for $9 million in revenue bonds](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1962/31558-0.html) to acquire four transit companies, build a central garage and offices, and get a public transit system running. The bonds and the seaport appropriations drew on different sources of money. Both projects required public officials to assemble the land, financing, and services that made a journey possible. The pier could get a visitor away for a week. The bus could get a resident to work every Monday morning. A longshoreman might depend on both.[^14]

At the other end of the voyage, the Bahamian government dredged Nassau Harbour and built a pier. [A Commonwealth survey](https://comsec-web-static.s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/ilibrary/pdfs/549.pdf?VersionId=6DIKu7LuCVYFpfg1bg2BvGrpd.UGQNhy#page=149) put the cost of dredging and pier construction at about $20 million. On February 25, 1969, the minister of transport opened the new 1,227-foot pier. Large ships that once anchored outside and sent passengers in by boat could now come alongside. Across the Bahamas, officials counted 362,071 cruise passengers that year. More than five hundred travel agents from the United States and Canada came on familiarization visits.[^15]

Nassau was already a cruise destination when the *Yarmouth Castle* caught fire on the way there from Miami in November 1965. [The Coast Guard inquiry](https://ncsp.tamu.edu/reports/USCG/yarmouthcastle.pdf) traced the fire to room 610. The watchman missed the passageway where it lay. A sprinkler alarm sounded on the bridge and in the engine room, but the general alarm never sounded. The *Bahama Star*, another passenger ship, took survivors to Nassau. Ninety people lost their lives.[^16]

[A 1966 federal law](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-80/pdf/STATUTE-80-Pg1356.pdf) made safety part of the sales literature. For voyages within its scope, it required operators to disclose which safety standards a vessel met. Separate provisions required evidence that an operator could pay injury or death judgments and repay fares if a voyage failed to operate. A customer looking at a brochure could hardly inspect the ship&#8217;s wiring or the company&#8217;s books. The sales agent who offered passage now bore some responsibility for what the customer knew about the vessel.[^16]

The American cruise business took shape on two coasts. In late 1965 Stanley McDonald chartered Canadian Pacific&#8217;s *Princess Patricia* for cruises from Los Angeles to Mexico. He liked the ship&#8217;s name enough to borrow it for Princess Cruises. A Princess [dinner menu dated December 4](https://www.firstversions.com/2018/01/princess-cruises.html) offers diners a choice that included New York cut steak, chicken with dressing, and boiled ox tongue with caper sauce. On the Mexican Riviera you could have Sanka with dessert.[^17]

[McDonald recalled](https://www.princess.com/en-int/news/backgrounders-and-fact-sheets/conversation-with-stanley-b-mcdonald-founder-of-princess-cruises) one difficulty with the arrangement. The *Princess Patricia* lacked an onboard laundry. Passengers sent their washing ashore at Acapulco, and the clean clothes came back jumbled together. Staff put them out on tables in the lobby so travelers could identify their own things. Wilkinson&#8217;s idea of a home that traveled survived intact until somebody needed a clean shirt. Then it depended on a laundry in Mexico.[^17]

![Watercolor portrait of Princess Patricia, a low coastal passenger steamer with two funnels, seen from off the bow.](oip-image:essays/the-making-of-the-floating-palace/princess-patricia)

*Princess Patricia, the Canadian Pacific vessel Stanley McDonald chartered for Princess Cruises in 1965.*

On the Atlantic side, Norwegian shipowner Knut Kloster and shipping agent Ted Arison found work out of Miami for the *Sunward*, which previously carried holiday passengers between England and Gibraltar. Norwegian Caribbean Lines began operations in 1966. Its [brochure effective December 15, 1968](https://www.timetableimages.com/maritime/images/ncl69ua.jpg), offered three- and four-day cruises to Nassau from $85. In 1969 the ship would sail every Monday and Friday. A travel agent could match the visit to the customer&#8217;s days off. Anyone who missed the Friday sailing could go on Monday.[^18]

The brochure promised straw markets, a calypso band, and shops where a visitor could find the &#8220;treasures of the Continent&#8221; at bargain prices. It also promised that *Sunward* would dock right at Bay Street. The sentence turned the Bahamian government&#8217;s harbor work into a convenience a travel agent could sell. Eastern Steamship interests and Commodore also offered departures from South Florida. A travel agent shopping for a client already enjoyed a choice of ships.[^13],[^18]

![Watercolor portrait of Sunward crossing blue water, with a low white superstructure and a single funnel.](oip-image:essays/the-making-of-the-floating-palace/sunward)

*Sunward helped turn the short Caribbean cruise into a regularly scheduled holiday from Miami.*

The flag on the stern answered only one question about who ran a ship. [In 1960](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/372/24) the *Nassau* sailed from New York for the Caribbean under the Liberian flag. Italian shareholders owned the operating company. It hired its crews in Italy, where the men signed Liberian articles. When a union picketed the ship in New York, some crew members stopped work. Longshoremen and tug crews also withheld their services for a time. Several cruises were canceled.[^19]

In Florida the *Ariadne* sailed under Liberian registry and the *Bahama Star* under Panamanian registry. When they called at Florida ports, crew members shared the work of loading cargo and automobiles with men hired ashore. In May 1966, longshoremen from Local 1416 put a picket beside the vessels over dock wages. The operators secured a Florida court injunction. In 1970 [the U.S. Supreme Court reversed](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-397/pdf/USREPORTS-397-195.pdf) the Florida court&#8217;s judgment. Because the dispute concerned work on American docks, jurisdiction lay with the federal labor board. What the companies paid for loading a car mattered to Miami workers who lived on dock wages.[^19]

Royal Caribbean took a third route into the business. The partnership dates to 1968. Three shipping firms backed a new vessel built at Wärtsilä&#8217;s Helsinki yard specifically for this market. *Song of Norway* went into the water in December 1969. The yard delivered her in October 1970, and on November 7 she began her maiden voyage. Shipowner Gjert Wilhelmsen [told the design journal](https://web.archive.org/web/20260612053325id_/http://www.rooke.se/pdf/Tidsfasetter3.pdf#page=42) *Mobilia* that the partners set their sights on the middle ranks of society. Americans who once considered cruising beyond their means were precisely the people whose vacation habits he meant to change.[^20]

Wilhelmsen put the ship&#8217;s [construction cost at $16 million](https://web.archive.org/web/20260612053325id_/http://www.rooke.se/pdf/Tidsfasetter3.pdf#page=46). The specifications allowed for 880 passengers. Each passenger cabin got a telephone and a separate tap for iced water. The design coverage also shows a crew cabin, the ship&#8217;s nurses, and an automatic egg cooker in the galley. A passenger could turn a tap and get a cold drink without calling a steward. Down in the galley, the cooks faced the problem of several hundred people who might all want breakfast at about the same time.[^20]

![Watercolor view alongside Song of Norway: white hull, a blue band below the windows, open decks, and an angular bridge in warm light.](oip-image:essays/the-making-of-the-floating-palace/song-of-norway)

*Song of Norway, built specifically for Royal Caribbean&#8217;s cruise business and delivered in 1970.*

Ted Arison appeared again in March 1972, this time as [president of a cruise operation owned by the tour packager AITS](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/FSupp/669/1297/2370497/). Carnival chartered a former Canadian Pacific liner, the *Empress of Canada*, from another AITS subsidiary. Renamed *Mardi Gras*, the liner ran aground on a sandbar outside Miami on her maiden voyage. On paper the arrangements ran to the minute. The printer dated Carnival&#8217;s brochure February 25, 1972, down in the margin beside the advertising agency&#8217;s name.[^21]

[The cover offered](https://gettraveling.com/gfx/s/mar-01.jpg) seven days from Miami to St. Thomas, San Juan, and Montego Bay, starting at $175 subject to availability. Embarkation on Dodge Island ran from 12:30 to 2:30. The ship would leave every Saturday at three. A deposit of a quarter of the fare held the reservation. Meals came with it. Gratuities and shore excursions cost extra. A passenger who drove to the pier could even find the price of parking, $1.50 a day, payable to the attendant.[^21]

![Watercolor portrait of Mardi Gras, a former ocean liner with a long white superstructure and a red funnel, underway at sea.](oip-image:essays/the-making-of-the-floating-palace/mardi-gras)

*Mardi Gras, formerly Empress of Canada, began Carnival&#8217;s weekly departures from Miami in 1972.*

In 1867 a committee decided whether you could come. In 1972 you could pick a Saturday and put down a deposit. Behind that simple transaction stood shipyards, cooks, travel agents, and governments willing to build a pier at either end. Together they made a holiday that could run to a timetable. The ship would feed you, entertain you, and bring you home. Duncan needed one shipload of approved applicants to make his excursion work. Carnival needed another shipload every Saturday.

## About the book

*Swimming With Pigs* follows pleasure cruising from its early excursions to the modern industry, then examines the money, labor, rules, and public choices behind a week at sea.

By [Robert V. Ussley](https://outsideinprint.org/authors/robert-v-ussley/) · Outside In Print
