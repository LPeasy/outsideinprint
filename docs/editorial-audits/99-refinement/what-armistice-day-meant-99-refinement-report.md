## Editorial Philosophy Audit

Decision: PASS

- Evidence: PASS — The firsthand diary, contemporary papers, presidential texts, congressional resolution, House report and statutes support the corresponding scenes and institutional claims.
- Logic: PASS — The armistice accounts explain the date, the 1926 resolution records observance already underway, and the 1938 and 1954 acts establish the legal holiday and its later name. The Medford scenes illustrate civic practice without claiming they caused the federal change.
- Incentives: PASS — The sources identify why people gathered, why the Legion invited veterans across wars, and why Congress wanted to honor a much larger veteran population through an existing observance.
- Tradeoffs: PASS — The House report gives the concrete choice to expand an existing holiday instead of establishing another one. The final statute shows how Congress retained the dedication to peace while broadening whom the day honored.
- Consequences: PASS — The piece follows the practice from a battlefield hour through local commemorations to a recurring federal holiday and the 1954 change in whom it honored.
- Uncertainty: PASS — Shinn speaks for his unit. The Medford advertisement establishes an invitation. The 1945 report establishes who took part that day. The article confines its legal conclusion to the 1954 amendment.
- Institutional Behavior: PASS — The text distinguishes local organizers, state holiday decisions, presidential observance requests, congressional recommendations and binding legislation.

## Media Framing Audit

Decision: PASS

- Media Frame Identified: PASS — The period Portland paper's celebratory victory framing and the presidents' commemorative rhetoric are attributed to their speakers.
- Primary Source Rebuild: PASS — The diary and contemporaneous local press establish bounded scenes. The agreement and statutory record establish the terms and legal actions.
